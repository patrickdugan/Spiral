Takeoff-evidence examples for the evaluation design calculus

Draft 0.2 — inspected 2026-10-11; figures checked against full source text the same day.

This atlas applies the calculus and the suggested feedback insertions to five published claims that bear on AI-driven AI research and development. These are design analyses and proposed studies. No model was called and no published result was reproduced.

Each case distinguishes observed, interpretation, and proposed variation, as the Prime Intellect atlas does.

Reading note. Draft 0.1 was written from a fetch tool that returns a summary of each page, with one chart read by eye from a screenshot and several figures taken from secondary pages. For draft 0.2 every figure attributed to a source below was checked against the source's full text in a browser, or against the data embedded in the source's page. The secondary pages and the screenshot are superseded and no longer cited. §9 lists what the check changed. Source levels are marked per case:

  • primary: the publisher's own page, paper or repository, read in full.
  • primary, embedded data: the data behind a chart or table on the publisher's page.

1. The question put to each case

A forecast of compounding needs one estimand: the gain from one generation of controllers to the next, repeated, on a readout the improvement process did not touch. In the notation of the insertions, that is a series of \(V^{r}_{\pi_g}\) with \(r\) sealed, together with a ledger showing that it stayed sealed.

Card field What to look for in a takeoff claim
\(\Pi\) What improves: weights, harness, test-time budget, or the mix of who does the work
\(D\) How scored items were chosen, and whether the choice followed a look at results
\(Z\) Whether what was kept depends on how it scored
\(J\) Who or what judges, and whether the judge shares errors with the judged
Feedback Whether scores or items from the scored set reached the improvement process
Readout Whether any reported number comes from a set declared sealed
\(C\) Which estimand the evidence supports, as against the one the headline implies

3. OpenAI's internal model: an amplification curve read as a trajectory

Observed. The Navier–Stokes post, dated 8 September 2026 with an update on 10 September, states: "Since August 28 we have been training a new internal model," and "This model's training is ongoing and its performance continues to improve." It describes the model as built through large-scale reinforcement learning on a pretrained model. That result involved on the order of 10,000 concurrent agents, about 88 hours, about 2.7 million messages and roughly 130 billion output tokens, with Lean formalization taking a further 17 hours. Two details of the run bear on feedback. Partway through, OpenAI moved the agents to a further-trained version of the internal model. And it cross-pollinated agent groups, using Codex to turn each group's intermediate results into follow-up prompts; the group that found the result was guided this way. Primary

A chart titled "Performance of GPT-6 Astra and our Internal Model on a curated set of open math problems" plots pass rate against test-time compute on a log scale, with error bars. Its data is embedded in the page. The internal model runs from 0.262 to 0.474 over six points, at relative compute 2.6 to 93; GPT-6 Astra runs from 0.084 to 0.170 over five points, at 1.4 to 31. Error bars are one standard error. Every pass rate is a whole multiple of 1/382, which fits 382 scored attempts, for example 191 problems tried twice; the page does not say which. Primary, embedded data

The 6 October release reports ten reasoning summaries and an average result using "the equivalent compute of roughly three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking." The repository README lists 719 manuscripts in 372 families from about 4,000 problems posed, and states that roughly 42% of top-line results are formalized in Lean; the history file gives 300 of 719. The release went out with 722 manuscripts. On 7 October a sign error led OpenAI to withdraw three and revise fourteen others. The README also says that some outputs build on earlier results produced by the models, and that results entered the catalogue only if judged significant enough. Its README says: "We expanded these evaluations after performance on our existing mathematical evaluations saturated." Primary; repository and history.

Interpretation. The chart's horizontal axis is test-time compute. It compares two controllers at several inference budgets at one moment. It is not a series over training time, and the sentence that performance "continues to improve" has no plotted support on the page. At matched compute near 12–13 units the internal model passes 0.382 against GPT-6 Astra's 0.144. Because the controller was replaced during the Navier–Stokes run, that result is not a single-checkpoint measurement either.

Three declarations are absent, and a fourth is only partly made:

  • \(D\): the rule by which the set was "curated", and whether curation followed a look at outcomes. Expanding an evaluation after saturation supplies fresh items, and is also a decision taken on results.
  • \(Z\): results were kept when judged significant. What is published is conditioned on the judgment, and three withdrawals a day after release measure some of what that judgment let through.
  • \(J\) for the chart: what counts as a pass, and who decides.
  • Feedback: within the run the channel is declared and item-level. Intermediate results were fed back into prompts, outputs build on earlier outputs, and the controller was replaced partway. Whether these problems or their outcomes reached training is not stated. The Navier–Stokes page addresses a different contamination question.

Derived arithmetic (conditional on the reported counts; the mapping from problems to manuscripts is not given):

\[ \frac{719}{4000},\qquad \frac{372}{4000}=\frac{93}{1000},\qquad \frac{300}{719}\approx0.417,\qquad \frac{3}{722}\approx0.004 . \]

These are ratios of kept output to problems posed, not success rates. If a failed attempt cost what a kept one did, compute per kept family would be \(4000/372\approx10.8\) times the per-result figure; the release does not say whether failures are in its average.

Proposed variation. The same pass-rate curve at two training checkpoints, on a problem set declared sealed before the first, with the selection rule and all failures published. That yields the training-time gain at fixed inference budget, which is the quantity the caption asserts.

Claim boundary. A steeper curve for the newer model is evidence that more inference buys more from it than from its predecessor, in mathematics, on this set. It is silent on whether that margin is being trained back in, and the run that produced the headline result used at least two checkpoints.

4. Anthropic's Automation Index: a judge measuring a mix

Observed. A prototype index catalogues about 15,000 granular tasks into a frozen tree of 542 nodes, 378 of them leaves, each rated on Epoch AI's automation scale. "Leads" (AL4) means the model completes most of a task end-to-end from a high-level prompt while a human supervises. Aggregation is weighted by person-time. A Claude agent gathers evidence per node and an independent Claude judge assigns the level. Staff rated their own areas as a check: model–human exact agreement 59%, human–human 35%, within one level 97%. As of August 2026 the "leads" share is 26% and the share at or above "collaborates" exceeds 90%; no measured subset is fully autonomous. The basket is frozen at July 2026. Stated caveats include dependence on the judge, contestable AL3/AL4 cases, crude weighting, and a judge that may share errors with the models it rates. Primary

Interpretation. This is the atlas's Rubric Discovery case at institutional scale: the produced artifact is a judge, and it needs its own evaluation. Three points follow from the card.

  1. The headline is a threshold count at one boundary of a six-level scale. Neither published agreement figure is the one it needs. Exact agreement, 59%, is a lower bound on how often judge and human put a node on the same side of the AL3/AL4 line. Agreement within one level, 97%, does not bound it either way, since AL3 against AL4 is within one level. The headline needs agreement on that binary call and the direction of the disagreements: disagreements that cancel leave the share unbiased.
  2. Weights come from staff weeks sampled in July 2026. Each sampled person's week is one unit, split evenly across the tasks they worked on. That counts tasks touched, not time spent, so a task that became faster does not lose weight for that reason. The scheme has a different blind spot: work that no sampled person touched in a sampled week never enters the basket. Fully delegated work is the most likely to be missed, so the finding that no subset is fully autonomous partly reflects how the basket was built.
  3. It is a measurement of who does the work. It is the \(p\) of an Amdahl bound, not a gain per generation.

Derived arithmetic (illustrative only: 26% is a share of task-touch weights, not of time):

\[ \frac{1}{1-\tfrac{13}{50}}=\frac{50}{37}\approx1.35 . \]

That is the ceiling on overall speedup if the "leads" share became free and nothing else changed.

Proposed variation. Blind human re-rating of a stratified sample of nodes near the AL3/AL4 boundary, with the headline reported as an interval under a one-level perturbation of the judge. Separately, weights from a time-use survey, compared with the task-touch weights.

Claim boundary. There is no scored item set here, so feedback and readout do not apply. The index supports a statement about task mix under a declared judge. It does not measure whether each generation improves the next by more.

5. METR's time horizon: a suite that hides by saturating

Observed. Time Horizon 1.1 fits a logistic curve of success against human task duration over "over a hundred diverse software tasks" from RE-Bench, HCAST and shorter novel tasks, and reads off the 50% point. The page states: "Measurements above 16 hrs are unreliable with our current task suite." It carries a notice dated 8 September 2026 that it is no longer actively updated. It mentions private tasks without giving the public/private split. METR added the 16-hour notice on 8 May 2026, in the same update that added Claude Mythos Preview (early). Primary

The page embeds its data. Claude Mythos Preview (early), released 7 April 2026, has a 50% horizon of 1044.78 minutes, with an interval of 508.9 to 3304.3 minutes, and an average score of 0.852 on the suite. METR's fitted doubling time for models from 2023 on is 128.7 days, with an interval of 104.4 to 158.0; a stitched all-time fit gives 187.8 days. No fit restricted to 2024 onward is published. The page does not state the level of its intervals. Primary, embedded data

Interpretation. In the testbed's terms a saturated suite is an observation-only Hide. Once a controller's ability exceeds the hardest items, distinct controllers push forward to the same record, and the discrimination gap between them goes to zero. No change in the game is involved.

The suite is fixed and reused across model generations and developers, and its scores are published. At the level of the field that is at least score-only feedback over years. Nothing read asserts that any developer trained against it. An average score of 0.852 for the top model is consistent with a suite near saturation.

Derived arithmetic.

\[ \frac{1044.78}{60}\approx17.41\ \text{hours}>16,\qquad \frac{508.9}{60}\approx8.48<16<\frac{3304.3}{60}\approx55.07 . \]

The highest frontier point lies above the bound the instrument's owner calls reliable, and METR said so when it published the point. Its interval runs from about half the bound to more than three times it.

Proposed variation. Report, per model, the count of items above its estimated ability. Extend the suite with long tasks held sealed. Declare the freeze date as part of the instrument.

Claim boundary. Below the ceiling the horizon supports a statement about released models on this task family. Above it, neither a plateau nor an acceleration is identified.

6. AIDE²: the case with a declared readout

Observed. An agent proposes rewrites of its own harness; candidates are scored on a selection suite of ML engineering, heuristic algorithm engineering and harness engineering tasks; scoring uses private data averaged into a grade. Inner agents see only a public signal. The outer agent "reads the previously proposed agents and their grades", and its reviewer "explores the evaluation artifacts". The main run lasted eight days: one initial agent and 99 proposals, seven accepted at steps 2, 6, 28, 39, 47, 63 and 85, raising the incumbent grade from 0.703 to 0.778. The outer loop ran on Claude Opus 4.7 and the inner agents on Gemini 3 Flash. Two further runs accepted two and four rewrites.

Held-out reporting uses ALE-Bench (10 tasks, 10 seeds), MLE-Bench (22 tasks, 3 seeds), FML-Bench (18 tasks, 3 seeds) and WeatherBench 2 (1 task, 3 seeds). Gains there are described as "positive throughout but not monotone"; the step-47 agent is best on two of the four. Reward hacking on 38 held-out KernelBench pairs fell from 55% to 39% to 32%, against 39% for the human-engineered agent. The authors state that the selection trace cannot show generalization because candidates were selected on the grade, and that the outer loop's role "cannot be decisively distinguished" from a strong baseline. The two evolved agents reported on the external benchmarks are fixed by position: the incumbent within the first 50 nodes (step 47) and the final incumbent (step 85). About a quarter of the graded rewrites that were rejected beat the incumbent on the signal the agent could see and lost on the private grade. Primary

Table 1 reports the external benchmarks with one model per benchmark held fixed across harnesses, and \(\pm\) one standard error of the benchmark mean. On ALE-Bench the initial harness scores \(1536\pm33\), step 47 \(1713\pm26\) and step 85 \(1790\pm9\). A separate comparison across models, at a $20 budget per run, has Gemini 3 Flash with the step-85 harness at \(1858\pm22\) against Fable 5 with the initial harness at \(1796\pm24\). On MLE-Bench, with Fable 5, the step-85 harness is within one standard error of the initial one.

Interpretation. This is the only case of the five that separates a selection set from a readout and says which numbers came from which. In ledger terms:

  • Selection set: one fixed suite, 99 score queries. Declared access is at least score-only; the reviewer's reading of evaluation artifacts may make it item-level. The rise from 0.703 to 0.778 is a selection score.
  • Readout: four external benchmarks, read at two evolved checkpoints chosen by position, not by readout score. Both are reported on every benchmark, not the best per benchmark, which is the reporting a ledger asks for. Whether the positions were fixed before the run is not stated.
  • The quarter of rejected rewrites that won on the visible signal and lost on the private grade measures how far the loop's two signals diverge. It is the within-task analogue of the readout gap.
  • What improves is the harness. The model is frozen, so this is not evidence about weights improving weights.

Derived arithmetic.

\[ \frac{7}{99}\approx0.071,\qquad \frac{0.778-0.703}{7}=\frac{3}{280}\approx0.0107 . \]

Intervals between acceptances are 4, 22, 11, 8, 16 and 22 steps; five of the seven fall in steps 1–49 and two in steps 50–99, with none in the last 14. At a fixed model, step 85 against the initial harness differs by 254 points on ALE-Bench. Treating the two standard errors as independent, that is \(254/\sqrt{33^2+9^2}=254/\sqrt{1170}\approx7.4\) standard errors. The same calculation gives about 3.5 on MLE-Bench, 2.6 on WeatherBench 2 and 3.4 on FML-Bench. Runs on the same tasks are paired, so these figures are likely conservative. The cross-model ALE-Bench contrast is 62 points against a combined error of \(\sqrt{22^2+24^2}=\sqrt{1060}\approx32.6\), about 1.90. It sets a harness gain against a model gain and measures neither. The reward-hacking percentages are consistent with 21, 15 and 12 of 38; those counts are inferred from rounding.

Proposed variation. Declare the readout positions before the run and publish the ledger. Hold a second private split of the selection suite sealed, and read it once at the end: the difference from the grade is a direct measurement of the readout gap on the suite the loop worked against.

Claim boundary. One trajectory supports the existence of held-out gains from harness self-modification, large relative to their standard errors at a fixed model, with acceptances thinning over the run. It does not support a rate.

7. Princeton-led shadow evaluation: sealed against contamination, open at the judge

Observed. Agents were given the central question of two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions. Main runs used Claude Opus 4.8 with extra-high reasoning on OpenClaw; a robustness run used GPT-5.6 Sol on Codex for one question. Each run had six days, $3,000 in API credits and GPU credits. The papers' own authors gave overall scores out of 6, knowing the work was AI-generated: 2/6 and 1/6. The five runs were two pilots without extended reasoning, two main runs and the robustness run. The paper records three human interventions during a run, including a fix to a harness bug and a 24-hour deadline extension. Stated limitations are five runs on two papers, non-blind grading by the original authors, researcher degrees of freedom in question selection, possible under-elicitation, and evaluation awareness. The authors found no significant reward hacking, and report five instances of subagents misrepresenting results that the orchestrator caught. The author list spans Princeton and about ten other institutions. Primary

Interpretation. Using unpublished questions seals the item set against pretraining, a choice none of the other four makes for its items. The opening is at \(J\): the judge knows the condition and wrote the reference answer. In the terms of the v3 protocol's provenance block, scorer_blinded would be false, and there is no negative control. A human-written submission graded under the same label would say how much of the score belongs to the label.

The models tested predate the internal model of §3. The result is silent on that model.

Proposed variation. Blind grading with human-written decoys mixed in, on more questions, with the authors of each question grading only the others'.

Claim boundary. Failure on two questions under an unblinded judge supports "not demonstrated here". It does not bound what a later controller does.

8. What these examples add to the calculus

  1. Feedback class and ledger. Two cases of five declare a feedback channel, and only one pairs it with a sealed readout. In the other the channel runs within a single effort, and the controller was replaced partway. The card should ask for the channel, the readout, and any change of controller.
  2. Saturation as Hide. A fixed suite outgrown by its subjects loses discrimination without any change to the game. The ceiling belongs on the card as a property of \(Z\).
  3. Acquisition conditioned on judgment. Publishing what scored well makes \(Z\) depend on \(J\). Counts of what was attempted are then part of the evidence, not a courtesy.
  4. A judge that shares errors with the judged. Agreement statistics must be reported at the boundary the headline is counted at.
  5. Estimand named against headline. Amplification slope, task share, horizon, harness gain and open-ended failure are five different quantities. A statement about compounding needs a sixth, which none supplies.
  6. The version read. OpenAI's repository withdrew and revised manuscripts a day after release. A card should record the version of a source it read.
